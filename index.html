The problem.

Generic drugs carry most of America's prescriptions. Every one of them reaches patients through an ANDA, and a large part of every ANDA is careful, repetitive writing.

9 in 10

US prescriptions are filled with generics.

Source: FDA
600

new ANDAs were filed with FDA in fiscal 2025.

Each one carries its own labeling and regional documents.

Source: FDA statistics
30–60

pages in a typical prescribing label.

Every word has to be accounted for in the generic version.

the same as the labeling of the reference listed drug except for differences annotated and explained What US law requires of every generic label, 21 CFR 314.94(a)(8)

Word for word, by hand.

Regulatory teams copy the reference label, change what the law allows, and write a reason for every difference, mostly in Word.

One miss can cost months.

A protected use left in, or a cross-reference left pointing at removed text, can draw an FDA deficiency and delay approval.

It never stays done.

Every time the brand label changes, every generic that follows it has to be updated and re-annotated.

Our solution.

Software does the volume. People keep the judgment. Scroll to see how one section is made.

  1. Research

    Regulark gathers what the section depends on: the reference label, Orange Book patents and exclusivities, and FDA's product-specific guidance.

  2. Draft

    It writes the section in FDA's vocabulary. Every sentence is traced back to the source it came from.

  3. Annotate

    Differences from the reference drug are marked and numbered, each with its reason, the way the annotated comparison is laid out.

  4. Approve and record

    A named person on your team approves each change. Every decision is chained into a record that shows if it's ever altered.

Your whole ANDA.
Starting with Module 1.

We start where the writing is most repetitive and the rules are most exact: the regional section and its labeling. Then we carry on through the rest of the dossier.

Module 1, where we start. The US regional section: cover letter, basis for submission, the bioavailability waiver request, and the labeling, including the annotated comparison with the reference drug.

Module 2. Summaries of the information in Modules 3 to 5, written to a fixed structure.

Module 3. Quality: the drug substance, the finished product, and how they are made and tested.

Module 4. Nonclinical study reports, where a filing needs them.

Module 5. Clinical study reports. For a generic, mainly the bioequivalence studies showing it matches the reference drug.
